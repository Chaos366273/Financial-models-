Traditional benchmarks barely move. 1/N, Min-Var, MVO and BL(No Views) all keep positive, respectable Sharpe ratios under realistic costs (0.41–0.50) because their turnover is low (0.07–0.16) — there's simply little to erode.
Ranking strategies collapse, they don't just shrink. Top-50 XGBoost's net return falls from 9.6% to 1.3% and its Sharpe falls to essentially zero (0.00) — monthly costs of 77bps (≈9.2% a year) consume almost the entire gross return. Gross returns barely move; it's the realistic cost drag that kills the result.
ML-BL is the cautionary tale of the table. Its turnover (0.64) is roughly 4–9× higher than every other strategy, and each unit of that turnover costs more too (name-level cost, not the flat 10bps academic assumption) — together that produces 121bps/month in costs (≈14.5% a year), turning a positive academic Sharpe (0.36) into the only negative net return (−5.5%) and a catastrophic −70.8% drawdown.

How to present this in your report

  • Keep Table 3.10 as an appendix table (for the reader who wants the exact numbers), but lead the main text with these charts — a professor scanning the section should get the finding from the shape of the chart in 2 seconds, not from reading 64 cells.
  • Lead with the dumbbell charts, not the bar chart. The "before → after" gap is your actual finding (statistical ranking skill doesn't survive realistic costs); the cost/turnover bar chart is the mechanism explaining why, so it works well as the second chart, right after you've shown the reader the effect.
  • One sentence per chart underneath, not a full paragraph. You already have strong analytical prose written (the text you pasted) — keep that in the body copy, and let the chart carry the "wow, look how far that fell" moment on its own.
  • Colour-code consistently across all three charts (blue = traditional, orange = ML, red = ML-BL) so your professor's eye learns the code once and reuses it instantly on the next chart.
