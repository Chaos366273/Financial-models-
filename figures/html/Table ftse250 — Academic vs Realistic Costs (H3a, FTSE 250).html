How FTSE 250 differs from FTSE 100 (Table 3.10)

On FTSE 100, Top-50 Ensemble was already the strategy your text flagged as vulnerable, but it wasn't the academic winner. Here on FTSE 250, Top-50 Ensemble is the single best academic result in the whole table (Sharpe 0.79, beating every traditional benchmark) — which makes its collapse to Sharpe −0.12 under realistic costs the most dramatic reversal in either table. The underlying reason is liquidity, not the model: FTSE 250 has a much wider average bid-ask spread (31.5bps vs 7.5bps) and a 4× higher Kyle impact coefficient (0.02 vs 0.005), so the same turnover costs far more here than on FTSE 100.
Traditional benchmarks again survive. 1/N and BL(No Views) stay positive under realistic costs (7.4% and 8.3% net) because their turnover (0.08–0.09) barely triggers the wider FTSE 250 spreads.
The academic "winner" becomes a loser. Top-50 Ensemble's net return falls from 13.3% (the best in the table) to −0.4% — a complete reversal, not just a smaller edge. Gross returns (~14.5%) are almost unchanged; 124bps/month of realistic cost (≈14.9% a year) is what erases it.
This is a capacity problem, not an alpha problem. The ranking signal is real (that's why the academic Sharpe of 0.79 would be publishable) — but at a £10m NAV, the mid-cap liquidity needed to actually trade on that signal isn't there. ML-BL is the extreme case: turnover of 0.61 at FTSE 250's wider spreads produces 163bps/month, turning a small academic edge (Sharpe 0.10) into the worst result in either table (−15.0% net, −91.8% drawdown).
